% Chapter 3: Related Work
\chapter{Related Work}\label{ch:related}

\guide{Discuss the studies closest to your work, grouped by approach. For each
group, say what was done, how well it worked and what is still missing. End
by stating the gap that this thesis fills, and show how your work is similar
to, yet different from, previous work.}

Deep video saliency models differ mainly in two respects: how they represent
the temporal dimension of a video, and what their encoder has been trained on
before it meets eye-tracking data. This chapter follows that development, from
recurrent models built on image features (\cref{sec:related-a}), through
three-dimensional convolutional encoders (\cref{sec:related-3d}) and
transformers (\cref{sec:related-b}), to models that build on video foundation
encoders (\cref{sec:related-vfm}). Audio-visual models, which add the
soundtrack, are reviewed separately (\cref{sec:related-av}), as is the small
body of work that treats prediction error as a cue for attention
(\cref{sec:related-surprise}). The chapter closes with a summary and the research
gap that this thesis addresses (\cref{sec:gap}).

\section{Recurrent and Two-Stream Models}\label{sec:related-a}

The first deep video saliency models extended image saliency networks with
mechanisms for motion and memory. Bak et al.~\cite{bak2018spatio} used
two-stream networks, one stream on the frames and one on optical flow, and
compared ways of fusing them. Lai et al.~\cite{lai2020video} coupled appearance
and motion streams more tightly through dense residual cross connections and
added a convolutional gated recurrent unit for long-term dynamics. Gorji and
Clark~\cite{gorji2018going} took the opposite route and augmented a strong
static saliency model with recurrent \enquote{attentional push} pathways, which
model how the photographer and the actors in a scene direct the viewer's
attention.

Recurrence on top of per-frame convolutional features became the dominant
design. DeepVS~\cite{jiang2018deepvs} combined an object-to-motion network with
a saliency-structured convolutional \acrshort{LSTM}, motivated by the observation, made
on its own large eye-tracking dataset, that moving objects attract gaze.
ACLNet~\cite{wang2018revisiting,wang2021revisiting}, introduced together with
DHF1K, added a supervised attention module that encodes static saliency to a
\acrshort{CNN}-\acrshort{LSTM}, so that the recurrent part can concentrate on temporal change; it was
trained with static images from SALICON alongside the videos. Linardos et
al.~\cite{linardos2019simple} showed that a simple exponential moving average
of features can perform on a par with a convolutional \acrshort{LSTM}, which suggests that
much of the temporal modelling in these networks amounts to smoothing.
UNISAL~\cite{droste2020unified} pursued a lightweight encoder--\acrshort{RNN}--decoder
design trained jointly on image and video saliency data, with domain-adaptive
priors, fusion and smoothing to cope with the differences between datasets,
and reached strong results at a fraction of the size of its competitors.

These models share a limitation: their spatial features come from networks
pretrained on images, and their temporal modelling is confined to a recurrent
state on top of those features. Their reliance on static saliency data, which
helped them markedly~\cite{wang2021revisiting}, is a sign that the video data
alone were not sufficient to train them.

\section{Three-Dimensional Convolutional Encoder--Decoders}\label{sec:related-3d}

A second generation replaced per-frame features by spatio-temporal features
from three-dimensional convolutional networks pretrained for action
recognition, typically S3D~\cite{xie2018rethinking} trained on
Kinetics~\cite{carreira2017quo}. TASED-Net~\cite{min2019tased} encodes a clip of
consecutive frames and decodes the features spatially while aggregating the
temporal dimension, producing one map per clip; maps for a whole video are
obtained by sliding the window, under the assumption that a limited number of
past frames suffices to predict the current one. HD2S~\cite{bellitto2021hierarchical}
supervises intermediate \enquote{conspicuity} maps at several levels of
abstraction and adds domain adaptation through gradient reversal and
dataset-specific priors. ViNet~\cite{jain2021vinet} decodes features from
several levels of an action-recognition encoder with trilinear upsampling and
three-dimensional convolutions; it is causal, runs in real time and, despite
using no audio, outperformed audio-visual models on six audio-visual datasets.

Later work in this family focused on efficiency and on the choice of encoder.
TinyHD~\cite{hu2023tinyhd} combines several simple heterogeneous decoders with
hierarchical map distillation from a larger teacher, and predicts several
output frames per forward pass to reduce cost. MSFF-Net~\cite{zhang2024msff}
fuses multi-scale spatio-temporal features through a bi-directional pyramid
with attention-guided fusion. Most recently, ViNet-S and
ViNet-A~\cite{girmaji2025minimalistic} paired a lightweight decoder with either
an S3D encoder or a SlowFast encoder pretrained for spatio-temporal action
localisation; their ensemble, ViNet-E, reported the best results among the
compared methods on UCF Sports and Hollywood-2. The strength of the
action-localisation encoder on these two datasets, whose gaze was recorded
during action recognition~\cite{mathe2015actions}, illustrates how much the
pre-training of the encoder matters.

Three-dimensional convolutions capture motion well, but their receptive field
in time is limited by the kernel sizes, and their encoders were trained for
classification on a fixed set of action categories.

\section{Transformer-Based Models}\label{sec:related-b}

Self-attention relaxes the locality of convolutions. STSANet~\cite{wang2023spatio}
inserts spatio-temporal self-attention modules at several levels of a
three-dimensional convolutional backbone to capture long-range relations
between time steps, and fuses the levels with an attentional multi-scale
module. VSFT~\cite{ma2022video} was the first purely transformer-based model in
the field and extended the task to forecasting the saliency of future frames.
TMFI-Net~\cite{zhou2023transformer} builds on multi-level features of a video
transformer, enriches them top-down with high-level semantics in a
semantic-guided encoder, and decodes them hierarchically with attention along
the channel, temporal and spatial dimensions. THTD-Net~\cite{moradi2024transformer}
argues that the temporal dimension of transformer features should be kept high
throughout decoding, and reaches results comparable to multi-branch decoders
with a single-branch one. UniST~\cite{xiong2025unist} uses one saliency-aware
transformer for both video saliency prediction and video salient object
detection, and TM2SP~\cite{li2025tm2sp} combines a Video Swin
encoder~\cite{liu2022video} with a multi-level spatio-temporal feature pyramid
guided by a pretrained image saliency encoder.

These models raised the published DHF1K test correlation from about 0.52 to
about 0.55~\cite{moradi2024salfom}. They remain, however, trained end to end,
or with an encoder pretrained for supervised action recognition, on the few
hundred videos of a saliency dataset, and they are large: TMFI-Net has about 61
million parameters and STSANet about 169 million~\cite{girmaji2025minimalistic}.

\section{Video Foundation Models for Saliency}\label{sec:related-vfm}

Foundation models trained with self-supervision on large video collections
offer a different starting point. SalFoM~\cite{moradi2024salfom} was the first
video saliency model built on such an encoder. It fine-tunes the \acrshort{UMT}-L
encoder~\cite{li2023unmasked}, a \acrshort{ViT}-L trained by masked video modelling guided
by an image--text teacher, together with a heterogeneous decoder that includes
a locality-aware spatio-temporal transformer. Given 16 frames at
$224\times224$ pixels, it predicts the saliency map of the last frame, and
maps for a whole video are produced with a sliding window. SalFoM reported the
best DHF1K test results among the methods it compared with (\acrshort{CC} 0.569, \acrshort{NSS}
3.353), but it was weaker than earlier models on UCF Sports, which its authors
attribute to the task-driven gaze of that dataset and to overfitting on its
small training set~\cite{moradi2024salfom}.

The same group has since combined foundation encoders with explicit
long- and short-term temporal modelling~\cite{moradi2026lstd} and distilled them
into smaller models to reduce their cost~\cite{moradi2026knowledge}. A
different direction couples a multimodal large language model with a saliency
decoder to produce saliency maps together with textual explanations, trained on
a new dataset of gaze annotated with reasons~\cite{wu2026explainable}.

Two properties of these approaches are relevant here. First, the encoder is
adapted to the saliency data, either by full fine-tuning or by distillation;
whether a frozen foundation encoder is sufficient, and how it compares with an
adapted one, was not examined in the studies whose full texts could be
consulted. Second, the sliding-window design runs the large encoder once for
every output frame, which makes inference expensive.

\section{Audio-Visual Saliency Models}\label{sec:related-av}

Because most videos have sound, and sound influences where people
look~\cite{coutrot2012influence,coutrot2014how}, a separate line of work fuses
the soundtrack with the frames. STAViS~\cite{tsiami2020stavis} combined a
spatio-temporal visual saliency network with auditory features, learned to
localise sound sources, and fused the two saliencies; it also introduced the
protocol, with three folds per dataset, under which six audio-visual datasets
are now evaluated. CASP-Net~\cite{xiong2023casp} addresses the temporal
inconsistency between the two modalities with a consistency-aware predictive
coding module, and DiffSal~\cite{xiong2024diffsal} formulates prediction as
conditional generation with a diffusion model conditioned on audio-visual
features. Models based on implicit neural representations map space--time
coordinates to saliency values and fuse the modalities
parametrically~\cite{zhu2024discrete,zhang2025audio}, and the relevance-guided
fusion of Yu et al.~\cite{yu2026relevance} adjusts how much audio is retained
according to its semantic relevance to the visual content.

Whether these models actually use the audio is disputed. Jain et
al.~\cite{jain2021vinet} found that an audio-augmented variant of ViNet became
agnostic to its audio input after training and observed the same behaviour in
an earlier audio-visual model. Agrawal et al.~\cite{agrawal2022does} examined a
range of audio modules and fusion techniques on ten datasets and found that none
improved on visual-only models. On several audio-visual datasets, visual-only
models such as ViNet-E report results that equal or exceed those of
audio-visual models~\cite{girmaji2025minimalistic}.

\section{Prediction Error as an Attention Signal}\label{sec:related-surprise}

The idea that attention is drawn to what violates expectation has a long
history. Itti and Baldi formalised \enquote{surprise} as the divergence between
an observer's prior and posterior beliefs, detected surprising events in video
with low-level feature models~\cite{itti2005principled}, and showed that this
measure predicted human gaze shifts better than the other metrics they
tested~\cite{itti2009bayesian}. Predictive-coding accounts of the visual
cortex propose that neural responses signal the error of predictions made from
context~\cite{rao1999predictive}, and deep networks built on this principle have
been trained to predict future video frames~\cite{lotter2017deep}.

Modern self-supervised video models contain an explicit predictor. In the
joint-embedding predictive architectures I-\acrshort{JEPA}~\cite{assran2023self} and
\acrshort{V-JEPA}~\cite{bardes2024revisiting}, a predictor network learns to predict the
representations of masked regions from the visible ones. Such a predictor
offers, in principle, a learned and high-level analogue of surprise: its error
indicates which parts of a scene the model could not anticipate. In the
literature reviewed for this thesis, the error of such a learned latent
predictor has not been evaluated against human fixations. CASP-Net uses
predictive coding in a different sense, to align audio and visual
features~\cite{xiong2023casp}.

First, progress in video saliency prediction has tracked progress in the
encoder: from image networks with recurrence, through action-recognition
three-dimensional networks, to video transformers and, most recently, video
foundation models. The decoders, by contrast, have converged on variations of
multi-scale fusion. Yet the encoders used for saliency have all been trained
with supervision or with objectives that reconstruct or align tokens, and they
have been adapted to the saliency data. Encoders trained with a
feature-prediction objective, such as \acrshort{V-JEPA}~\cite{bardes2024revisiting}, whose
authors report that its features perform well without adapting the model's
parameters, have not been evaluated for this task in the peer-reviewed studies
whose full texts could be consulted.

Second, the evaluation practice of the field makes small differences hard to
interpret. DHF1K results are reported on the validation or the test set,
published numbers for the same model differ between papers by up to 0.03
\acrshort{CC}~\cite{girmaji2025minimalistic,xiong2025unist}, and comparisons are almost
always made against numbers copied from other papers rather than against
re-scored predictions with an uncertainty estimate.

Third, the idea that motivated this work, that a learned video model's
prediction error marks where people look, has support in classical models of
surprise~\cite{itti2009bayesian} but had not been tested with the predictor of a
modern self-supervised video model.

This thesis addresses these gaps. It evaluates a frozen \acrshort{V-JEPA}~2 encoder with a
light decoder that predicts many frames per pass, compares it with SalFoM, the
closest foundation-model approach, under one validated evaluator with
paired statistics, tests the encoder's own predictor as a source of attention
cues, and examines which design choices still matter once the encoder is fixed.
It resembles SalFoM in building on a video foundation encoder and shares its
multi-scale decoder design with much of the field; it differs in keeping the
encoder frozen, in the objective with which that encoder was trained, in the
multi-frame output, and in the pre-registered experimental protocol.
