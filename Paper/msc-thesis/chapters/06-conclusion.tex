\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\guide{Summarise the problem, what was done and the main findings, and answer
each research question briefly. Do not introduce new results here.}

This thesis asked whether the representations of a self-supervised video
encoder trained by feature prediction are sufficient for video saliency
prediction without adapting the encoder, and whether the encoder's own
prediction error indicates where people look. To answer these questions, it
developed SURGE-D, which combines a frozen \acrshort{V-JEPA}~2 encoder with a light decoder
that reads four intermediate layers, rebuilds a multi-scale pyramid, decodes it
with factorised spatio-temporal convolutions and predicts saliency maps for
sixteen frames per forward pass. Each component that was tested was evaluated
under a rule written in a dated log before the corresponding experiment, all
models were scored once on their test sets by a single evaluator validated
against reference code, and differences were assessed with paired statistics.
The answers to the research questions are as follows.

\paragraph{RQ1.} With the larger encoder, yes. On the DHF1K validation set, the
frozen \acrshort{V-JEPA}~2 \acrshort{ViT}-g model reached a correlation coefficient of 0.600 as a
single model and 0.603 as a three-seed ensemble, against 0.564 for SalFoM, the
closest foundation-model approach, scored by the same evaluator, and was better
on all six metrics with paired bootstrap intervals that exclude zero. Every seed
stayed ahead when SalFoM's maps were given the blur that suits them best. Under
an identical protocol, with both models restricted to past frames and scored on
the same frames, the causal \acrshort{ViT}-g ensemble was more accurate than SalFoM by
0.029 and as accurate as the offline model, so the advantage does not come from
future context. With the \acrshort{ViT}-L encoder, the size of SalFoM's, only the
ensemble was reliably better, offline and under the identical protocol, where
its margin was 0.017. The gain comes from the \acrshort{V-JEPA}~2 encoder rather
than from freezing as such, because a frozen \acrshort{UMT}-L encoder with the same
decoder scored below SalFoM. In offline use, SURGE-D with \acrshort{ViT}-g needs
about a ninth of SalFoM's encoder computation per output frame and, in the
released implementations, less than a twentieth of its time; the causal
variant needs nearly twice the computation but less time. These are
validation-set results; the official test-set evaluation was pending when the
thesis was written.

\paragraph{RQ2.} The prediction error of the released \acrshort{V-JEPA}~2 predictor
carries information about where people look, but not in the way that the
surprise hypothesis predicts. On its own, it scored below frame difference and
token atypicality at both frame rates tested and was anti-correlated with
fixations, with bootstrap intervals over the 60 holdout videos that exclude
zero. This held with the trained mask token as with the untrained one of the
first run, and for a readout that fills in masked tubes as in pre-training.
Combined with the centre prior and the two simple cues, the error added 0.069
and 0.062 bits per fixation in the two comparisons fixed in advance, with a
negative weight: observers looked less often where the error was high. The
finding concerns one released predictor and two ways of reading it out; it
does not refute surprise-based accounts of attention.

\paragraph{RQ3.} With a frozen encoder, the encoder itself dominated. Its
pre-training (\acrshort{V-JEPA}~2 over \acrshort{V-JEPA}~2.1 and
\acrshort{UMT}-L) and its scale (\acrshort{ViT}-g over \acrshort{ViT}-L)
produced the only large gains, and seed ensembles a small one. Over three
seeds, removing the learned centre prior or deep supervision was equivalent to
keeping them, within $\pm0.003$ \acrshort{CC}, and low-rank adaptation of the encoder lost
in every seed. Static-image co-training, 32-frame clips and 11\,\% more training
videos produced no gain that could be established, and a sparser temporal
stride, which halves the cost per output frame, improved with every seed
without reaching the margin. Whether the amount of training data, rather than
the capacity of the decoder, limits further progress was tested with a learning
curve and remains open: holdout \acrshort{CC} rose with every doubling of the
training set between 135 and 540 videos, but the last doubling, a gain of
0.005, was inconclusive under its rule.

\paragraph{RQ4.} The approach transferred well after brief fine-tuning, within
the limits of comparisons across evaluation code. It was competitive with the
best published results on UCF Sports. On the audio-visual datasets, its ensemble
reached a higher correlation than the best peer-reviewed results that could be
verified on four of the six datasets, tied on a fifth and was lower on the
sixth. These margins are of the same order as the disagreements between
published evaluations of the same models, and the comparison is limited to
peer-reviewed results, so it does not establish a state of the art. Its clear
failure was on recordings of
conversations, where gaze follows the speaker, which an image-only model cannot
identify. Adding the soundtrack through a simple fusion module did not improve
any dataset, in line with earlier findings that audio-visual saliency models
tend not to exploit the audio.

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:final-contributions}

The thesis makes four contributions. First, it presents SURGE-D, a video
saliency model with a frozen self-supervised encoder and a 7-million-parameter
multi-frame decoder, which was more accurate than a fine-tuned foundation-model
approach on the DHF1K validation set, both offline, at a fraction of its encoder
computation, and as a causal model under an identical evaluation protocol.
Second, it reports a negative result, tested under rules written in advance:
the error of the released \acrshort{V-JEPA}~2 predictor is anti-correlated with
fixations and, combined with simple motion and appearance cues, adds
information only with a negative weight, so it does not behave as a surprise
signal that attracts gaze. Along the way, it shows that the predictor of a
widely used model-loading library reads by default a mask token that
pre-training never updated. Third, it provides controlled evidence about
which design choices matter
once the encoder is frozen: the encoder's pre-training and scale dominate,
while two components commonly included in saliency models, a learned centre
prior and deep supervision, are equivalent to their absence over three seeds,
and the low-rank adaptation of the encoder that was tested did not help. Fourth, it
contributes an evaluation methodology, with a validated evaluator, decision
rules fixed in advance, paired statistics and a reproducible ground truth for
Hollywood-2 built from openly licensed gaze recordings, that makes its positive
and negative findings verifiable.

\section{Future Work}\label{sec:future}

\guide{Describe the limitations of the work and the directions that follow
from them.}

The limitations of the study point to several directions for future work.

\paragraph{Completing the evaluation.} The DHF1K test-set scores of the
prepared predictions and an evaluation on the standard Hollywood-2 ground truth
would place the results on the benchmarks' official footing. Both depend on the
dataset authors and were pending when the thesis was written.

\paragraph{Strengthening the ablations.} With three seeds, two of the four
component ablations remained inconclusive. A temporal stride of four, which
halves the cost per output frame, improved holdout \acrshort{CC} with every seed without
reaching the margin, and is the most useful one to settle, with more seeds or on
a larger evaluation set; static-image co-training is the other. The encoder
comparison and the clip-length test used one seed each, and the 32-frame model
was still improving at the end of its schedule, so it merits a longer training
run under a new rule written in advance. The learning curve ended at the 540
training videos that DHF1K provides, and its last step was inconclusive; more
seeds, or training videos from a second dataset, would show whether the amount
of data limits progress.

\paragraph{Adapting the stronger encoder.} Low-rank adaptation was tested only
with \acrshort{ViT}-L, and full fine-tuning not at all. Whether adaptation helps the larger
\acrshort{ViT}-g encoder, or helps once more training data are available, remains open.
The \acrshort{V-JEPA}~2.1 encoder should also be compared at the resolution at which it
was trained.

\paragraph{Speaker-aware audio-visual prediction.} The failure on conversations
calls for models that determine who is speaking, for example by explicit
localisation of the active speaker, rather than modulating visual features with
a global audio embedding.

\paragraph{Efficient causal prediction.} A causal model needs one forward pass
per output frame, so the saving that SURGE-D gains from predicting sixteen
frames per pass does not carry over to streaming use: the causal \acrshort{ViT}-g model
needs nearly twice SalFoM's encoder computation per output frame, and the
causal \acrshort{ViT}-L model, which needs about two thirds of it, was reliably more
accurate only as a three-seed ensemble. Cheaper causal designs, for instance
with shorter windows or windows that forecast several frames ahead, remain to be
tested, as does causal prediction on datasets other than DHF1K.

\paragraph{Revisiting surprise.} The error of the released predictor carries
information about fixations with the opposite sign to the hypothesis, and what
it measures that draws gaze away is not known. A first step is to describe the
regions where the error is high, for instance by the amount of texture, motion
or object boundaries that they contain, and to test whether the negative weight
survives when such properties are added to the simple cues. Predictors trained
explicitly to extrapolate into the future, or surprise measured as a change of
belief relative to the scene's own history, may behave differently, and the
incremental-information analysis of this thesis could test them directly.

\paragraph{Evaluation practice.} Scoring each metric with the map that is optimal
for it under the model's prediction~\cite{kummerer2018saliency}, and reporting
paired statistics against re-scored baselines, would make comparisons in the
field more informative.
